Incident Pulse Documentation

Reading Incident Pulse like an analyst

This isn't a button-by-button manual — it's about turning the numbers on screen into decisions: where backlog is really building, whether a team is genuinely struggling or just catching harder tickets, and which single root cause is quietly driving your incident volume. Every section below leans on one running example — a fictional retail IT desk, Meridian Retail Group — so the numbers stay connected across tabs, the way real interpretation actually works.

Getting Started

What Incident Pulse actually does

Incident Pulse turns a raw ServiceNow incident export into an operational story: how much is coming in, how fast it's being cleared, who's carrying the load, and where the same problem keeps resurfacing. It never talks to a live system — everything you see is computed, in your browser, from the CSV you upload.

Most incident tools stop at "here's a table." Incident Pulse's job is the layer above that: it pre-computes the comparisons an analyst would normally build by hand — this priority against that one, this team against the estate average, this week against last week — so you can spend your time on why a number moved, not on building the pivot table that reveals it moved.

💡
Core ideaEvery screen answers one question. Executive Overview asks "how are we doing right now." Volume & Trends asks "is it getting better or worse." SLA & Aging asks "what's at risk." Group Performance asks "who owns that risk." Categories asks "what's actually causing it." Keep that question in mind as you read each tab and the numbers stop being a wall of KPIs and start being an argument.

What this guide will not do

It won't tell you that the Choose File button uploads a file, or that clicking a tab switches the view. If a control's purpose is obvious from its label, it's skipped here on purpose — the space is spent on the part that isn't obvious: what a metric means, when it's trustworthy, and what a healthy vs. unhealthy pattern actually looks like.

Click any entry to jump straight there, or just scroll; the currently-visible section highlights automatically. Use the search box (or press /) to filter the list down to a keyword like "MTTR" or "bounce."

A few recurring boxes will show up throughout:

💡

📊

Getting Started

Loading and scoping your data

Everything in Incident Pulse flows from one CSV export, dropped onto the upload box at the top of the screen. There's no fixed template — the app scans your header row for recognizable ServiceNow-style column names (Number, Opened, Priority, Assignment group, Made SLA, and so on) and maps what it finds. Columns it can't find simply fall back to sensible defaults, which matters more than it sounds:

  • Missing "Made SLA" column → every incident is treated as having no SLA target, so it's excluded from SLA Compliance entirely rather than counted as a breach or a pass. A 0% or 100% SLA figure on a freshly uploaded file is usually a column-naming mismatch, not reality — check SLA & Aging against a manual count before reporting it.
  • Missing "Location" → defaults to "Main Campus." If most of your rows show that value, the location column wasn't picked up.
  • Missing resolve-time field → MTTR falls back to Resolved − Opened timestamps, which is a reasonable proxy but won't match a "business hours only" duration field if your ServiceNow instance tracks one.
⚠️
Watch out forEvery metric in this app is computed only from the rows currently in scope. If your export only covers "still open" tickets, MTTR and SLA compliance will look artificially perfect — there's no failure population left to drag them down. Always confirm your export includes both open and resolved/closed incidents before trusting a KPI.

The date scope & filters

The SCOPE date range at the top of the workspace, plus the group / priority / state / channel filters, apply to every tab and both overlays simultaneously — there's one shared "current view," not a separate filter per screen. This is why a number can look different in Executive Overview between two visits: someone narrowed the scope. Before comparing two sessions, glance at the SCOPE bar and the Validation Checks strip on Executive Overview (covered next) to confirm you're both looking at the same slice.

Tab 1 of 7

Executive Overview

The question this tab answers: "If I only had thirty seconds, what's the state of the desk right now?" Everything here is a snapshot, not a trend — for movement over time you want Volume & Trends instead.

The KPI Summary

Six tiles, each clickable — clicking one jumps straight to the tab that explains it further (e.g. clicking SLA COMPLIANCE opens SLA & Aging). Read them as three pairs, not six independent numbers:

  • Total Incidents paired with Open Backlog — volume vs. what's still unresolved. A high open percentage against a low total isn't the same risk as a high open percentage against a high total; always read the backlog share, not just the count.
  • MTTR paired with SLA Compliance — speed vs. whether that speed is fast enough. A dropping MTTR with a dropping SLA rate usually means priority mix shifted (see the callout below) rather than the desk actually slowing down.
  • Unique Callers paired with Avg Open Age — how broad the demand is vs. how stale the backlog is getting.
⚠️
Watch out forMTTR is an average across every resolved ticket regardless of priority. If Critical incidents made up 4% of last month's resolutions and 9% this month, MTTR can rise even though every single priority band individually got faster — the mix changed, not the performance. Cross-check against SLA & Aging → SLA Compliance by Priority before concluding the desk slowed down.

The Breakdown view

Toggle from KPIs to Breakdown (top-right of the left panel) to swap the six tiles for three ranked lists: State, Priority spectrum, and Top Contact People. This is the fastest way to answer "what does this month's ticket mix actually look like" without leaving the tab — useful before a standup, when you want composition rather than headline numbers.

The Generated Output

The right-hand panel isn't decorative — it's a plain-text operational update, built live from your current scope and filters, meant to be copied straight into an email, a Teams message, or a status doc. It always follows the same shape: totals, then SLA and MTTR, then the top category and top ticket template, then reopen rate.

📊
Worked exampleMeridian Retail Group, 1–31 August. Total logged: 1,842. Resolved/Closed: 1,605 (87.2%). Open Backlog: 237 (12.9%). SLA Compliance: 90.0% (169 breaches). MTTR: 6h 42m (1,605 cases). Avg Open Ticket Age: 4.6 days (54 older than 7d). Unique Requesters: 612 (168 repeat callers). Top Category: Fault (612 tickets, 33.2%). Top Template: POS Terminal Offline (214 tickets, 11.6%). Reopened Incidents: 61 (3.3% reopen rate).

Read that last line first when triaging a month: an 11.6% share on one single ticket template, out of dozens of possible templates, is a strong signal — one recurring, nameable problem is worth a fifth of the entire month's volume.

Expand Calculation rules underneath the textbox any time you need the exact formulas without leaving the tab — the same three are covered in more depth in the Metrics Glossary later in this guide.

The Validation Checks strip

Three small chips — SCOPE, SLA STATUS, RESOLVED — sitting under the KPI grid. They're a sanity check, not a metric: SCOPE confirms how many rows are actually in play, SLA STATUS turns amber once compliance drops under 90%, and RESOLVED confirms the closed count. Glance here first whenever a number elsewhere looks surprising — it's the fastest way to rule out "I'm looking at the wrong date range" before you go looking for a root cause that isn't there.

Tab 2 of 7

Volume & Trends

The question this tab answers: "Is the desk getting ahead of the work, or falling behind it?" Executive Overview gives you a still photo; this tab gives you the film reel. Every point on every chart here is clickable and opens that day's (or hour's) breakdown drawer.

MTTR Resolution Trend

Plotted by resolved date, not opened date — a ticket logged on the 3rd and closed on the 9th shows up in the 9th's average, because that's when it actually finished consuming effort. Switch Daily MTTR to Cumulative MTD to see the running month-to-date average instead of the single day's — cumulative smooths out noisy single-day spikes and is the better view for "are we trending better or worse this month," while daily is better for "what happened on that one bad Tuesday."

💡
TipA single-day MTTR spike is almost always one or two very old tickets finally closing, not a sudden slowdown. Click the spike to open its breakdown and check whether it's driven by a handful of long-aged closures rather than a real bad day across the board.

Incident Inflow Volume

Plotted by opened date. Daily mode shows raw intake — good for spotting weekday/weekend rhythm and one-off spikes (a store outage, a patch rollout gone wrong). Cumulative mode shows the running total for the month — good for pacing against a target ("we're at 1,100 by day 20, on pace for roughly 1,700 by month end") and for comparing this month's trajectory shape against last month's at a glance.

Resolution Velocity (Created vs. Resolved)

Two lines, same axis: incidents logged per day against incidents resolved per day. This is the single best chart on the whole dashboard for answering "is our backlog growing or shrinking" — read the gap between the lines, not either line alone.

Net Delta = Total Logged − Total Resolved (in scope)
Burn-down Ratio = Total Resolved ÷ Total Logged
PatternNet DeltaWhat it means
Resolved line consistently above LoggedNegativeBacklog is shrinking — the team is working down older tickets faster than new ones arrive.
Lines roughly tracking together≈ 0Steady-state — inflow and outflow are balanced; backlog size stays flat.
Logged line consistently above ResolvedPositiveBacklog is growing — this is the earliest warning sign of an aging problem, well before it shows up in SLA & Aging.
📊
Worked exampleMeridian Retail Group, August: Inflow 1,842 · Outflow 1,605 · Net Delta +237 · Burn-down 87.2%. The desk is under-clearing by 237 tickets against this month's demand alone — which is exactly the size of the current open backlog. If Net Delta stays positive for a second consecutive month, backlog compounds rather than resets, and it's time to check Group Performance for which team is absorbing the overflow.

Intake Rhythm & Shift Radar

Two small charts answering a staffing question, not a performance one: 24-Hour Intake Density shows when in the day tickets arrive, and Day of Week Intake shows which days run heaviest. Use these to align shift coverage to actual demand rather than a guess — if intake peaks 10am–12pm and 3–5pm on weekdays, that's when you want your strongest first-line coverage, not evenly spread across the day.

Tab 3 of 7

SLA & Aging

The question this tab answers: "What's actually at risk right now, and how bad is it?" Where Executive Overview gives one blended SLA number, this tab breaks it apart by priority and turns the open backlog into an aging spectrum you can act on.

SLA Compliance by Priority

SLA Compliance (per priority) = Incidents Met SLA ÷ Incidents With an SLA Target

A blended 90% compliance figure can hide a serious problem: if Critical tickets are breaching at twice the rate of Low-priority ones, the blended number still looks fine because Critical is usually a small share of total volume. Always check this panel before declaring SLA "healthy" — a single-digit percentage-point compliance gap on Critical matters far more than the same gap on Low.

📊
Worked example
PriorityTrackedMetCompliance
1 – Critical968992.7%
2 – High41035887.3%
3 – Moderate98089090.8%
4 – Low20418490.2%
High-priority tickets are the weakest link here at 87.3%, below even Moderate — worth a direct look at which group owns High-priority routing before it's buried inside next month's blended 90.0%.

Open Backlog Aging Spectrum

Every currently-open incident, bucketed by how long it's been open: <2d, 2–7d, 7–14d, 14–30d, >30d. This is a shape, not a single number — a healthy backlog is front-loaded (most tickets in the <2d and 2–7d buckets); an unhealthy one has a long tail stretching into 14–30d and beyond, which usually means those tickets are stuck, not just slow.

⚠️
Watch out forA handful of very old tickets in the >30d bucket rarely represent "normal work still in progress." They're far more often waiting on something external — a vendor part, a change window, budget approval. Open each one in the Critical Aging & Stale Backlog table below and check the incident drawer's Work Notes before assuming it's a resourcing problem.

Critical Aging & Stale Backlog table

The full list of open tickets, oldest first, with an aging badge (green under 2 days, amber 2–7 days, red past 7). Click any row to open its full incident detail — caller, assignment history, work notes — without leaving the tab. Export Backlog CSV pulls exactly what's on screen, which is the fastest way to hand a "please chase these" list to a team lead.

Tab 4 of 7

Group Performance

The question this tab answers: "Which team is carrying which load, and is a ticket getting stuck between teams rather than inside one?" Two sub-views live here — Group Scorecards for straightforward team comparison, and the Reassignment Loops & Flow Matrix for something most incident tools never surface at all.

Group Scorecards

One row per assignment group: Volume, Open, MTTR, SLA, Reopened, and Reassignments. Read this table across, not down — a group with high volume and high MTTR isn't automatically underperforming if their SLA compliance is still solid; harder ticket categories legitimately take longer. The columns that actually flag risk are SLA (color-coded red under 90%) and Reassignments relative to Volume — a group reassigning a third of its tickets is a routing problem, not a speed problem.

📊
Worked example
GroupVolOpenMTTRSLAReassigns
IS - CG Service Desk940883h 10m94.2%140
IS - CG Network Ops410749h 55m78.6%260
IS - CG POS Engineering3205111h 20m71.4%205
IS - CG Field Services1722414h 05m68.9%96
Network Ops and POS Engineering both sit well under 90% SLA — but Network Ops handles 410 tickets against POS Engineering's 320, so Network Ops's shortfall costs roughly 30 more breached tickets in absolute terms even at a slightly better rate. When two groups both look "bad," rank by breach count, not just percentage, to decide where to intervene first.

Reassignment Loops & Flow Matrix

Switch the VIEW toggle to see this. It exists to answer a question the scorecard table can't: not "is this group slow," but "is a ticket bouncing between groups before anyone actually owns it?" A bounced ticket accumulates dead time in every handoff, on top of whatever time each group spends actually working it — which is why bounced tickets consistently resolve slower than tickets solved on the first queue.

First Contact Resolution (FCR) = Tickets With 0 Reassignments ÷ Total Tickets
Bounce Rate = Tickets With ≥2 Reassignments ÷ Total Tickets
MTTR Multiplier = Avg MTTR of Bounced Tickets ÷ Avg MTTR of 0-Hop Tickets
  • Total Transfers (Hops) — every reassignment across every ticket in scope, summed.
  • Reassignment Loops (≥2 Hops) — tickets bounced more than once, i.e. genuinely stuck in a routing loop rather than a single reasonable handoff.
  • First Contact Resolution — the inverse of bouncing: tickets the first group owning them also closed.
  • Bounce MTTR Multiplier — how much slower a bounced ticket resolves versus one solved on the first queue. Use the Bounce Depth filter above the matrix to tighten or loosen what counts as "bounced" for this comparison.
📊
Worked example612 total hops across 1,842 tickets. Reassignment Loops (≥2 hops): 158 tickets — an 8.6% bounce rate. First Contact Resolution: 1,232 tickets, 66.9%. Bounce MTTR Multiplier: 2.7x (2h 55m for FCR tickets vs. 7h 55m for bounced ones). That multiplier is the number worth repeating in a status meeting: a bounced ticket at Meridian takes nearly three times as long to close as one solved where it landed — independent of how "hard" the issue itself was.

The Group Transfer & Flow Matrix heatmap shows exactly who hands off to whom (rows = origin queue, columns = destination), with the darkest cells marking your busiest handoff corridors. The High-Hop Bounced Incidents panel beside it lists the worst individual offenders — click through to Incident Explorer to see them as full records.

💡
TipIf one corridor dominates the matrix — say, Service Desk → Network Ops consistently darkest — that's usually a triage problem, not a Network Ops problem: tickets are being logged or first-routed to the wrong queue and correcting course later. Fixing the intake categorization at the Service Desk often does more for MTTR than adding headcount downstream.
Tab 5 of 7

Categories, RCA & Channel

The question this tab answers: "What is actually causing all these tickets — and is it one thing wearing many names?" This is where volume stops being a number and starts being a fixable problem.

Ticket Templates & Issue Archetypes

Raw ServiceNow templates are often near-duplicates of each other — "POS Terminal Offline," "POS Terminal Not Responding," and "Terminal Down – POS" are very likely the same real-world issue logged three different ways by three different agents. Toggle Exact Templates to see them counted separately (useful for auditing template hygiene), or Smart Master Clusters to have similar templates merged into one group (useful for finding the true top issue). Click any template — or its Linkage indicator — to see exactly which raw names were merged into a cluster.

⚠️
Watch out forReading only Exact Templates can badly understate a problem's true size. Three templates at 80, 75, and 60 tickets each look like three separate mid-size issues — but as a Smart Cluster they're one 215-ticket issue, easily your single biggest category. Always check the Smart Cluster view before deciding a problem "isn't big enough" to prioritize.

The six classification panels

Categories, Subcategories, Contact/Channel, Resolution RCA, Resolution Codes, and Configuration Items — six ranked lists that each slice the same ticket population a different way. Used together, not separately, they tell a causal story:

Category tells you the surface symptom

e.g. "Fault" — a broad bucket that alone doesn't explain much.

Resolution RCA tells you the underlying cause

e.g. "Hardware Failure" — cross-reference against Category to see which symptom maps to which cause most often.

Top Configuration Items tells you where

if the same CI (a specific POS model, a specific router) keeps appearing, the fix is asset-level, not process-level.

📊
Worked exampleTop Category: Fault (612, 33.2%). Top RCA: Hardware Failure (365, 19.8%) followed by User Error (410 across all categories, but concentrated in Access, not Fault). Cross-referencing: most Fault tickets resolve to Hardware Failure, and the Top Configuration Items panel shows one POS terminal model responsible for 41% of those. That's a single asset-refresh decision, not 612 individual support tickets.

Incident Flow (Sankey)

Switch VIEW to Incident Flow for a Sankey diagram — three stages, connected by ribbons whose thickness is ticket volume. This is the tool for "walk me through how a ticket travels," end to end, in one glance. Nine ready-made presets cover the common questions (Category → Group → Root Cause, Priority → Group → SLA Performance, Channel → Category → Group, and others); Custom 3-Stage Flow Builder lets you pick any three fields yourself.

📊
Worked examplePreset: Category → Group → Root Cause. The thickest ribbon on the chart runs Fault → IS - CG POS Engineering → Hardware Failure — visually confirming, in one diagram, what the classification panels showed in three separate lists: POS hardware failures are this month's dominant story, and POS Engineering is the group that owns fixing it.

Use the Density control (Top 5 / 7 / 10) to reduce clutter on a busy dataset, and click any node to highlight just its flows — handy when presenting one specific pathway rather than the whole diagram.

Tab 6 of 7

Caller & Contact Intelligence

The question this tab answers: "Is demand broad-based, or is it a small number of people (or sites) generating a disproportionate share of tickets?" A high repeat-caller rate is often the earliest, cheapest-to-fix signal on the whole dashboard — it usually means one recurring issue, not one hundred separate ones.

Repeat Rate = Callers With >1 Incident ÷ Unique Callers
📊
Worked exampleUnique Callers: 612. Repeat Callers (>1 incident): 168 → 27.5% repeat rate. Roughly one in four people who contacted the desk this month did so more than once. That alone doesn't confirm a systemic problem — but paired with Top Frequent Callers showing several store staff logging 6–9 tickets each, almost all tagged "POS," it points straight back to the same hardware issue surfaced in the Categories tab.

Top Frequent Callers table

Sorted by incident count, with each caller's top category and location attached. Click any row to filter Incident Explorer down to just that person's tickets — the fastest way to check "is this genuinely one recurring issue, or five unrelated ones from the same busy person."

Distribution by Location & Channel

Two supporting panels: which sites generate the most contact volume, and which channel (phone, self-service portal, email, walk-up) people use to reach the desk. A site appearing disproportionately in both this list and the Location Intelligence overlay's alert list is worth a site visit, not just a ticket review.

Tab 7 of 7

Incident Explorer

The question this tab answers: "Show me the actual tickets behind that number." Every chart, KPI, and table elsewhere in the app is an aggregate — this is where you go to verify one, or to hand someone a concrete list instead of a percentage.

Every column header sorts (click again to reverse direction), the search box matches against number, description, caller, agent, configuration item, and location simultaneously, and pagination keeps large exports responsive — set Rows per page to All only when you actually intend to scroll a long list, since large tables render more slowly. Export CSV respects whatever search and column sort is currently applied, not the full dataset — useful for handing a filtered subset to someone without them needing to filter it themselves.

The incident detail drawer

Click any row (here or almost anywhere else clickable in the app) to open the full record: Issue Summary, Requester & Location, Assignment & Classification, Resolution & Root Cause, and — when present in your export — the full Work Notes activity trail. The header strip also surfaces two things not visible anywhere else in the dashboard:

  • SLA Status — MET, BREACHED, or N/A (no target was ever attached to this ticket, so it isn't counted in SLA Compliance either way).
  • Vendor Hold — flags a ticket whose delay traces to a third-party or vendor dependency rather than the assigned group's own pace. Useful context before holding a team accountable for an MTTR that was actually spent waiting on a vendor part.
💡
TipWhenever a chart elsewhere in the app looks surprising, the fastest gut-check is to open Explorer, search for the relevant term (a category, a group name, a date), and skim a handful of actual tickets. Aggregates can mislead in ways that three or four real records usually clarify in seconds.
Deep-Dive Overlay

Analytics & Insights

Opened via Executive Overview → Insights. This is the people-focused layer that sits above the tabs — where the seven tabs describe the ticket estate, this overlay describes who's handling it.

Top Performers leaderboard

Three ranked columns: Top SLA Compliance, Most Resolved Cases, and Coaching Opportunities. The third list matters more than the first two — it surfaces agents whose SLA rate or MTTR sits meaningfully below their peers, which is a conversation starter, not a verdict. Always check individual ticket complexity (via Explorer) before treating a coaching-list appearance as a performance issue rather than, say, a run of unusually hard tickets.

Heatmap — Date vs. Hour

Volume density across every day and hour in scope in one grid — the compressed version of Volume & Trends's Intake Rhythm chart. Darker cells mean busier windows; scanning for a consistent dark column (same hour, every day) confirms a staffing pattern, while a single dark cell flags a one-off spike worth investigating on its own.

📊
Worked exampleA consistent dark band at 10–11am and 3–4pm, Monday through Friday, with Monday running darker than any other weekday — a classic "weekend backlog clears into Monday morning" pattern. That's a staffing-schedule finding, not an incident-cause finding.

Caller & Contact Intelligence (deep view)

Metrics glossary & formulas

Every core formula used across the app, in one place. Nothing here is an approximation — this is exactly what Incident Pulse computes.

SLA Compliance
Share of SLA-tracked incidents resolved within their target. Incidents with no SLA value at all are excluded from both sides of the fraction — they neither help nor hurt the rate.
Met SLA ÷ Has SLA Target
MTTR (Mean Time to Resolve)
Average resolution duration across resolved tickets only. Falls back to Resolved − Opened timestamps if no explicit duration field is present in the export.
Σ Resolution Duration ÷ Resolved Count
Backlog > 7d
Currently-open incidents whose Opened date is more than seven days before the latest date seen in the dataset. The stale-backlog headline number.
Count( Open AND Age > 7 days )
Avg Open Age
Average age, in days, of every currently-open ticket — not resolved ones. Rises steadily whenever inflow outpaces outflow.
Σ Open Ticket Age ÷ Open Count
Repeat Rate
Share of unique callers who logged more than one incident in the current scope — the earliest signal of a recurring, fixable issue.
Callers with >1 Ticket ÷ Unique Callers
Reopen Rate
Share of incidents that were closed and then reopened at least once — a proxy for resolutions that didn't actually fix the problem.
Incidents with Reopens > 0 ÷ Total
First Contact Resolution (FCR)
Share of tickets resolved by the very first group they were assigned to, with zero reassignments.
0-Hop Tickets ÷ Total Tickets
Bounce Rate
Share of tickets reassigned two or more times — a genuine routing loop rather than one reasonable handoff. Adjustable via the Bounce Depth filter.
Tickets with ≥2 Hops ÷ Total Tickets

Color in Incident Pulse is never decorative — every one of these means the same thing everywhere it appears, across every tab and both overlays.

Status & health colors

Amber / WatchA metric trending the wrong way but not yet critical — e.g. a ticket aged 2–7 days, or a site above average volume but below the alert threshold.

Worked example: finding a real insight

This is the workflow the rest of the guide has been building toward — how an analyst actually moves through the app to go from "something feels off" to a specific, actionable finding. Same Meridian Retail Group dataset throughout.

Start at Executive Overview

SLA Compliance reads 90.0% — technically at the healthy line, but the Validation Checks strip shows SLA STATUS in amber, meaning it's right on the edge. Worth a closer look rather than moving on.

Drill into SLA & Aging

SLA Compliance by Priority shows High-priority sitting at 87.3% — the actual weak point hiding inside that blended 90.0%. The Aging Spectrum also shows a longer-than-usual tail in the 14–30d bucket.

Check Group Performance

IS - CG Network Ops and IS - CG POS Engineering are both under 90% SLA, and both show high reassignment counts relative to volume. Switching to the Reassignment Loops view confirms a 2.7x MTTR multiplier on bounced tickets — a real routing problem, not just two slow teams.

Find the root cause in Categories, RCA & Channel

Smart Master Clusters reveal "POS Terminal Offline" as the single largest ticket cluster at 214 tickets, resolving mostly to "Hardware Failure," concentrated on one Configuration Item — a specific POS terminal model.

Confirm the blast radius in Location Intelligence

Switching to Stores by Issue and selecting "POS Terminal Offline" shows Store 087 alone at 17 occurrences against a fleet average of 4–5 — the problem is concentrated, not evenly spread.

Land on the finding

Not "SLA is a bit soft this month" — instead: "High-priority SLA is being dragged down by POS hardware failures on one terminal model, disproportionately at Store 087, and the handoff between Network Ops and POS Engineering is adding a 2.7x MTTR penalty on top of the hardware issue itself." That's a finding with an owner, a cause, and a location — ready to act on, not just report.

Tips, shortcuts & FAQ

Shortcuts worth knowing

  • Esc closes whichever overlay or drawer is currently open — this guide included.
  • Any KPI tile on Executive Overview is clickable and jumps to the tab that explains it in more depth.
  • Almost every chart point, bar, and table row across the app is clickable and opens a breakdown or the full incident drawer — if it's colored or has a hover state, try clicking it.
  • Every export button (Export CSV, Export Backlog CSV, etc.) exports exactly what's currently on screen — respecting your active filters, search, and sort.

Frequently asked

Why does SLA Compliance show 100% right after I upload a file?

Almost always a column-mapping issue — your export's "Made SLA" column wasn't recognized, so no ticket has an SLA target and the formula's denominator is effectively empty. Check SLA & Aging against a manual spot-check, and confirm the column header in your CSV matches what ServiceNow exports by default.

Why did MTTR change when I only adjusted the date filter?

MTTR is calculated only from resolved tickets currently in scope. Narrowing the date range changes which resolved tickets are included — a range that happens to catch more long-running closures will show a higher MTTR even though nothing about the desk itself changed.

Two tabs seem to disagree on the same number — which one is right?

Both, usually — they're answering slightly different questions. Executive Overview's MTTR is a blended average across every resolved ticket; Group Performance's MTTR is scoped per group. Check exactly what population each figure is drawn from before assuming one is wrong.

What counts as "resolved" versus "open"?

The app reads the incident's State field — values like Resolved, Closed, Complete, or Done are treated as resolved; anything else (Open, In Progress, New, Pending, On Hold, Assigned) counts as open, unless a resolved date or duration is present despite an ambiguous state label.

Does anything here send my data anywhere?

No — every calculation in Incident Pulse runs locally in your browser against the file you uploaded. Nothing is transmitted elsewhere.
